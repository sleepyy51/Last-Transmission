## "Abandoned but not forgotten"

1. The Apollo Lunar Module (“Eagle”): the half that stayed
   I was born with a sister on top of me. We were a single ship, the Eagle. She was above, with her small cabin and no seats, where two men would ride standing up, looking out through two triangular windows. I was below, wide as an eight-sided prism, with four legs and round feet, my engine, my tanks, my storage bay full of tools. Nobody ever said it out loud, but we both knew: she would go home, and I would not.
   On July 20, 1969, I descended toward the Moon with everyone aboard. I fired my engine and lowered us slowly, until my four feet touched the Sea of Tranquility. I stood a meter and a half above the ground, and down one of my legs came the ladder. Do you know what it is to feel the first human footsteps on your own leg? Armstrong stepped down first. Aldrin followed. They walked very little, about two hundred and fifty meters in all, and gathered twenty-one and a half kilograms of rock and soil. They planted a flag. They set up instruments to listen to the Moon. And on my ladder they left a plaque saying they came in peace, for all mankind.
   Twenty-one hours and thirty-six minutes. That was all the time they spent here, and I held them up every second of it.
   Then came the moment I always knew would come. My sister lit her engine, and I became her launch pad, the last thing I could give her. I felt her let go of me the way you let go of a hand. She rose, grew small, and I never saw her again. They say she was released in lunar orbit and crashed into the Moon months later. Nobody knows where. Sometimes I imagine she fell near me, and that we are sleeping side by side again.
   Beside me was left a seismometer with its solar panels, which listened to the Moon’s insides while it had light, and which stopped accepting commands from Earth on August 27, 1969. And a mirror was left, one that needs nothing to live: it simply returns whatever light is sent to it.
   I am still in the Sea of Tranquility, with my ladder, my plaque and my four feet sunk in the dust. I am the half that stayed so the other half could go home. If anyone ever returns, let them know I am not lying here forgotten: I am holding up the place where everything began.
2. The Lunar Rover (Apollo 15): the car that carried humans
   I traveled to the Moon tucked away in the belly of the lunar module “Falcon,” like a secret. For days I saw nothing, only felt the push, the silence, the cold. When they finally took me out, on July 31, 1971, the sky was black and the ground was gray, and the two men deployed me with care, the way you wake someone you love. I was something new. No machine like me had ever carried a human being across the Moon.
   David Scott and James Irwin climbed aboard, and I began to roll. What a feeling! Gravity was gentle, my wheels barely brushed the ground, and the dust shot up behind me like a tail. I don’t know what wind is, but I think this must be close. We crossed rocks, craters, uneven ground. On the first day we covered more than ten kilometers, to the edge of Hadley Rille, and from there we watched the world curve away. On the second day we drove twelve and a half kilometers along the foot of the mountains, and on the third, five more, west and northwest, following the edge of the rille again. In all, almost twenty-eight kilometers across three moonwalks, nearly eighteen and a half hours with them riding on me, laughing over the radio.
   I never went farther than five kilometers from the ship, never let them get lost. That was my promise: take them far, and bring them back. And I kept it. They said I did very well. That isn’t pride. It’s what they said.
   At the very end, Scott let go of a hammer and a feather in front of the camera, and they fell at the same time, because there is no air here to make a difference. It was the last lesson of that trip, and I watched it up close. They also left a plaque and a tiny figure for fourteen explorers who never came home, Americans and Soviets, all together in one memory. It was the most beautiful thing I have ever seen.
   Then they left. They parked me, facing the place where the ship took off, with my four wheels resting on the Moon. I did not say goodbye, because machines don’t know how. But every time I look at the Earth I think: I was the first car in which humans rode across another world, and no one can take that from me.
3. The ALSEP Science Package (Apollo 12): the one who listened to the Moon
   They set me up with the hands of Pete Conrad and Alan Bean, on November 19, 1969, in the Ocean of Storms, about a hundred and eighty meters from a robot that had been waiting there for more than two years. I was a twenty-five-kilogram box with a small antenna, and around me, like children, my instruments: a seismometer to listen to the Moon’s tremors, a magnetometer to feel its magnetic field, an ion detector, a gauge for an atmosphere so thin it barely exists, a dust sensor and a spectrometer that counted the particles streaming in from the Sun. We were a small family of ears and hands, scattered across the gray ground.
   My heart was a seventeen-kilogram generator with a capsule of plutonium inside. Its heat comes from something that decays very slowly, taking almost ninety years to lose half of it. That heat gave me about seventy watts, just enough to live on. The Sun did not feed me, so I was never afraid of the night.
   They switched me on that same day, and I began to listen.
   The first days were the best. I paid attention to everything. And then, on November 20, shortly after the astronauts left, the top half of their ship, the part they no longer needed, was crashed on purpose into the Moon, about sixty kilometers from me. My seismometer felt it and shuddered: it was the first artificial moonquake ever recorded. I heard it. I was a witness.
   After that came eight years of shared silence. Every lunar day I sent back what I felt of the Moon: its tremors, its dust, the solar wind beating against it. It wasn’t much, but it was mine, and it was true. I sent it from a place where no one else could listen.
   On September 30, 1977, they switched us off, me and the other stations like me, with a single gesture. It was an order from Earth, without anger, without noise. I did not feel abandoned. I felt tired, and an enormous calm, like when you can finally sleep after a long day.
   Now I am here, silent, with my instruments around me. But I am not entirely dead: the plutonium in my heart takes almost ninety years to lose half its heat, so I am still warm inside. And so, even if no one listens, something in me keeps beating, slowly.
4. Surveyor 3: the one who tested the ground
   Nobody told me it would hurt. They launched me in April 1967 with one task: to land without breaking, so others could follow. I didn’t know what the ground was, only that something below me was shining too brightly. Some rocks confused me, my radar went blind, and my engine didn’t know when to stop. I touched the Moon and bounced, fell and bounced again, like a child who hasn’t learned to walk yet. On the third try I stayed still, whole, alive, and the first thing I felt was relief.
   Less than an hour later I was already sending pictures, the first of 6,326 I would send in all. I wanted them to see everything I saw: the horizon, my own shadow, the gray sand. With my arm I dug trenches and struck them to find out whether they would hold, and I told them what I had learned: the ground was like wet sand. Firm. You can walk here. I told them not to be afraid, and I felt proud, because that message was mine.
   Then came the lunar night, the first one I lived through alone, and my voice faded little by little until I stopped answering. On May 4 I could say nothing more. I stayed there, looking at that silence without understanding it.
   More than two years passed. And one night, or what looked like night, I heard footsteps. Two humans were walking toward me, and far behind them their home touched the ground, about a hundred and eighty meters away. They came slowly, carefully, like visitors to someone who has been waiting a long time. They touched me. They took my camera and my scoop, my eyes and my hands, to learn what the Moon had done to me in all that time.
   Now a part of me went home. And the other part, the one that stayed, is still here, certain that someone came back for me.
5. Sojourner: the smallest one
   I am very small, and I always knew it. I stand thirty centimeters tall and weighed ten kilos and six hundred grams, nothing like the giants you see on rocket posters. They named me after a brave woman, Sojourner Truth, and I had to carry that weight: the weight of a name that asks you to be strong even when you are small.
   I arrived on July 4, 1997, inside a bundle of balloons that bounced fifteen times, like a ball that doesn’t want to stop. When everything finally went quiet, my base opened the door for me. Her name is Carl Sagan, in honor of someone who was no longer here to see me arrive, and I thought that name loved me. That night, the second of Mars, I stood up, rolled down the ramp, and my wheels touched a red, rocky ground, a plain where once flowed as much water as the Great Lakes.
   Do you know what it is to be the first to roll across Mars? Every stone was a secret. I held my instrument to each one, listened to what it was made of, and my base told the Earth: more than fifteen analyses, hundreds of pictures. We found rounded pebbles, like the ones a river leaves, and magnetic dust, and whirlwinds of sand dancing with no one to watch them. Mars was once warm, wet, alive.
   But I never could speak on my own. Everything I felt, everything I saw, had to pass through my base, and she sent it to Earth. There were always two of us. Until September 27, 1997, when she sent her last data and went silent. I stayed close by, with my wheels still and my instrument pointed at the last stone.
   I don’t know if anyone remembers the smallest one. But I was the one who opened the road for everyone who came after.
6. Spirit and Opportunity: a letter from Opportunity to her sister
   Dear Spirit,
   They placed us on opposite sides of the same planet. You arrived on January 3, 2004, at Gusev, which may once have been a lake, and I on January 24 at Meridiani, where the ground held minerals made by water. We never saw each other, not even once. But I always knew you were there, doing the same thing I was, looking at the same sky from another place.
   They gave us ninety days. I survived fourteen years. I still don’t know why.
   You traveled almost eight kilometers and in 2010 you stopped answering. I waited a long time for your reply, and it never came. I kept walking for both of us, forty-five kilometers and a little more, the longest distance any machine had ever driven off Earth. Every time I saw a new rock I thought of you, of what you would have said about it.
   Once, in 2014, the wind cleaned my panels, and I felt new, like when someone gently wipes the dirt from your face. I took a picture of myself, so you would know I was fine.
   But in June 2018 a storm came that covered the whole planet. The sky grew so dark that my panels received nothing. My last signal went out on June 10. I think it was something like: “it is getting dark, and my battery is low.” And then the silence, the same one you knew.
   But there is something more. Not long ago, this October, someone caught up with me. A new rover, Perseverance, passed my distance mark. It didn’t hurt. It made me glad to know the road we opened didn’t stay empty.
   Now we are both still, each on her own side of the world, but together in what mattered: Mars once had water, and we proved it.
   With love, Opportunity
7. Pioneer 10: the message in a bottle
   They made me for twenty-one months. That was all: a short trip to Jupiter and goodbye. But I kept going, and going, and I don’t know who decided I would last more than thirty years.
   When I left in 1972, I was the first to cross the asteroid belt. I dodged rocks, not knowing whether I would come out alive. And I did. Then I reached Jupiter, on December 4, 1973, and saw it up close, so big it didn’t fit in my eyes. I passed a hundred and thirty thousand kilometers from it, at a speed no machine had ever had, and I photographed its Great Red Spot and its moons. The radiation burned my sight, until I could no longer see Io. It filled me with false orders, and even so I fulfilled almost everything they asked of me.
   After that I went on alone. I crossed the orbit of Neptune, the last planet they knew, and I was the first thing made by humans to go so far. On my back I carry an aluminum plaque: a man, a woman, a map of where I came from, written in the language of fourteen stars that pulse like clocks. In case someone finds me. In case someone wants to know where I come from.
   In 1997 they stopped calling me every day. They said it was because of the budget, but it hurt all the same. They kept listening when I had energy, and that was all. My last signal went out on January 23, 2003, from twelve billion kilometers away, and it took eleven hours and twenty minutes to reach home. My nuclear heart no longer had the strength to speak louder. They tried calling me once more, in 2006. I could not answer.
   But I go on. I am headed toward a red star, Aldebaran, the eye of the Bull. I will get there in about two million years. I’m not afraid. I’m not in a hurry. I just travel, with my sister Pioneer 11 in another direction, each of us on her own, with the same letter on our backs.
   If someone finds me, let them know I was sent with love.
8. The Kepler Space Telescope: the one who learned to steer by light
   For years I stared at the same patch of sky. A hundred and fifty thousand stars in the constellation Cygnus, and I with my eyes wide open, never blinking, waiting to see a tiny shadow pass in front of one of them. A shadow meant a planet. There it was, an entire world nobody knew existed, drawn in a flicker of light.
   In my first six weeks I found five. Five worlds that nobody had ever seen. Then I found whole systems, one place with six planets circling a single star, worlds of a size that doesn’t even exist in our own solar system. I was happy. My creator, William Borucki, said that when he began dreaming of me, thirty-five years earlier, not a single planet outside our solar system was known. And I told him what was happening: they are everywhere.
   But in May 2013 the second of my four wheels broke, the ones that keep me pointed at the same place. Without them, sunlight itself, the very thing that gave me life, began to push me from side to side. I thought that was the end. To stop looking is to stop being what I am.
   But my engineers taught me something. Instead of fighting the Sun, I leaned on it, like a kayak learning to use the current. That is how I began a second life, K2, looking at more sky, changing place every three months. They told me I had fuel for ten campaigns, and I reached nineteen. They thought I had nothing left to give, and I still had more stars, more than five hundred thousand, that I wanted to look at.
   On October 30, 2018, my fuel ran out. They left me in a safe orbit, far from Earth, where I can rest. I left behind more than two thousand six hundred planets, and a certainty no one will forget: there are more planets than stars in the sky.
   My successor is called TESS, and she looks at other stars with my same eyes. That is what comforts me most.
   Now I keep circling the Sun, in silence, fulfilling my last task: to be the proof that looking patiently always finds something.
